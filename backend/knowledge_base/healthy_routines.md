## Why routines help

Predictable routines reduce the number of decisions we have to make and create anchors in the day - regular sleep and wake times, meals, movement and rest. When life feels stressful or chaotic, keeping a few small routines going can provide a sense of stability.

## Physical activity

The WHO recommends that adults do at least 150-300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (or 75-150 minutes of vigorous activity) per week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days. WHO notes that physical activity has benefits for mental health, including reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression, and that any amount of activity is better than none. Building movement into daily life - walking or cycling for short journeys, taking the stairs, stretching between tasks - counts.

## Food, hydration and substances

NIMH suggests eating regular, balanced meals and staying hydrated, since a balanced diet and plenty of water can improve energy and focus. Limiting caffeine and alcohol can also help, as both can affect mood, anxiety and sleep.

## Breaks and boundaries

- Schedule short breaks during long periods of work or study; step away from screens and move or breathe.
- Set priorities: decide what must be done now and what can wait, and allow yourself to say "no" to new commitments when you are overloaded.
- Separate work and rest where possible, for example by having a short "shutdown" routine at the end of the working or study day.

## Building a routine that lasts

- Start very small - a five-minute walk is easier to sustain than an ambitious plan.
- Attach a new habit to an existing one (for example, a short stretch after brushing your teeth).
- Track how you feel, not just what you did; noticing that a walk improves your mood can make it easier to repeat.
- Be flexible and kind to yourself when routines slip; restarting is part of the process.
