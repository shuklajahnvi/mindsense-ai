## Why sleep matters for wellbeing

Sleep and mood are closely linked. Poor or short sleep can make people more irritable, less able to concentrate and more sensitive to stress, and stress and worry can in turn make it harder to sleep. Public health guidance from the CDC suggests that most adults need at least seven hours of sleep per night, while teenagers and children need more.

## Habits that support better sleep

- **Keep a regular schedule.** Going to bed and getting up at roughly the same time every day, including weekends, helps set the body clock.
- **Wind down before bed.** A relaxing routine - a warm bath, reading, gentle stretching or a breathing exercise - signals to the body that it is time to rest.
- **Make the bedroom sleep-friendly.** A dark, quiet, cool and comfortable room helps. Keep the bed for sleep rather than work.
- **Limit screens before bed.** Phones, laptops and TVs can be stimulating; try putting devices away some time before sleep.
- **Watch caffeine, alcohol and large meals.** Caffeine later in the day, alcohol and heavy meals close to bedtime can all disrupt sleep.
- **Get daylight and activity during the day.** Exposure to natural light and regular physical activity help regulate sleep, although intense exercise right before bed may make sleep harder for some people.
- **Write down worries.** If thoughts keep you awake, writing them down - or writing a short to-do list for tomorrow - before bed can help put them aside.
- **Don't lie awake for too long.** If you can't sleep, the NHS suggests getting up and doing something relaxing in dim light, then returning to bed when you feel sleepy.

## When to seek help

If sleep problems continue for weeks, affect daytime functioning, or come with symptoms such as loud snoring, gasping during sleep or extreme daytime sleepiness, it is worth speaking to a doctor or other health professional.
